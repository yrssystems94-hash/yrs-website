Settling A Local Question

Westmount is in both cities — on purpose

Ask which city Westmount is in and you'll get an argument, because the honest answer is both. The neighbourhood was assembled starting in 1912, when Talmon Henry Rieder bought 26 acres and the Westmount Improvement Company began acquiring land across Berlin, Waterloo and Waterloo Township. Plan 248 was registered in 1914 — Kitchener's first suburban residential development, laid out on City Beautiful principles with street names borrowed from Montreal's Westmount.

The City of Waterloo's own heritage study puts it plainly: Westmount is "a unique example of an upscale garden suburb that spans the Waterloo and Kitchener border," and notes that some houses are physically located in both municipalities. Each city designates its own Westmount heritage landscape. The 1914 marketing line — "the tie that binds the twin cities" — was literal.

A few practical notes that tell you whether someone actually knows the area. Locals call the portion east of Westmount Road "Old Westmount." The road changes name convention at the city line: it's Westmount Road East and West in Kitchener, North and South in Waterloo. And Westmount Golf & Country Club — Stanley Thompson's 1929 design, opened 1931 across 160 acres — is at 50 Inverness Drive on the Kitchener side, though its grounds cross the boundary. Inverness Drive was originally the club's entrance driveway; houses only appeared on it in the early 1970s.

We work both sides of the line. It's one neighbourhood.

The Part That Actually Matters

These rooflines are genuinely complicated

This isn't marketing language — it's the City of Kitchener's own heritage survey of the neighbourhood, which describes the dominant roof type directly:

"Rooflines are often complex, reflecting the Arts and Crafts architectural style, with typically one or more steep front gables intersecting with a gable roof."

And at the City's Westmount walkshop, residents themselves identified two features of their own housing stock: steep gable rooflines, and houses with jerkinhead roofs reflecting the area's German heritage. A jerkinhead — a clipped or half-hipped gable, where the peak is cut back at an angle instead of running to a point — is a genuinely distinctive Waterloo County form. It is also a real complication: the clip creates short, awkward hip runs and an unusual junction where the ridge meets the clipped face, and it has to be flashed and ventilated properly rather than approximated.

Every gable intersection is a valley. Every valley is a flashing detail that either gets built correctly or gets caulked and fails in six years. On a Westmount house, that's where the work is — not in stapling down field shingles.

The housing stock is 1½ to 2½ storeys, overwhelmingly brick in red and brown, predominantly Arts and Crafts with Tudor Revival and Colonial Revival influence, plus 1950s bungalow and ranch homes in the later western sections. Many were built by Reitzels Ltd., the local firm that worked here from the 1920s through the 50s and advertised two popular plans that recur throughout the neighbourhood. There are around 328 properties in Kitchener's east Westmount study area, and 466 across the full heritage landscape.

One thing we won't do is guess at your original roof material. The City's survey documents roof forms in detail and never names a material, so anyone telling you what Westmount roofs were originally clad in is speculating. We confirm it on site.

Roof Age, Honestly

Nobody in Old Westmount is on an original roof

Westmount filled in slowly. There were 9 houses by 1918, 44 by 1930, and roughly 65 before 1936 — held back by the First World War, Rieder's death in 1922, the Depression and then the Second World War. Kitchener's survey dates the core east of Westmount Road to a primary era of the 1920s and 1930s, with some 1950s and 60s infill. Land west of Westmount Road wasn't platted until the 1950s, Golfview Place and Orchard Park Crescent were complete by 1963, and Inverness Drive and Huntley Crescent — the final stage — went up in the early 1970s.

So the oldest homes here are 90 to 100 years old and on their fourth, fifth or sixth roof. That changes the job completely. On a century home the shingle is the least interesting part of the conversation. What matters is:

  • Decking that has taken multiple nailing cycles — sheathing that's been fastened through four or five times doesn't hold like new wood, and you only find out with the old roof off
  • Century-old chimney and sidewall flashing — masonry chimneys on these houses are original, and the flashing at the brick junction is where leaks in Westmount usually start
  • Ventilation that was never designed — a 1920s roof assembly predates any modern intake-and-exhaust thinking, and on a steep Arts and Crafts roof with finished space beneath, correcting it takes real thought
  • Layers — we tear off to the deck every time, which on a house this age is the only way to know what you actually have

The 1950s to 70s homes west of Westmount Road are a different market. Those may be on a first or second replacement, and a 1970s-era roof is now over 50 years old.

One caution if you're comparing quotes: MLS "Westmount" district averages include condominiums and townhouses on Westmount Road and Belmont, which pulls the number well below what a detached Old Westmount home is actually worth. Don't let anyone price your roof off a district average either.

The Canopy

Westmount's trees were written into the deeds

The tree canopy here is not an accident of time. Rieder's original deeds of sale required him to plant silver maples "at ten feet back from the street line and thirty feet apart" along John Street and Avondale Avenue. Boulevard trees went in during the 1930s. And because there is no overhead hydro on Rusholme Road, Union Boulevard, Glasgow Street, Dunbar Road, Claremont Avenue or Avondale Road, the City notes the trees have been able to reach "their full height and natural form." The planted medians on Rusholme, Union and Claremont create what amounts to a double canopy.

It's the neighbourhood's defining feature and it's hard on roofs. Constant organic debris into valleys and gutters. Moss and algae on slopes that stay shaded. Slower drying after every rain. And silver maples specifically are fast-growing and brittle — heavy limb and seed drop directly over roof planes is part of owning a house here.

Being straight with you about what Westmount isn't: it's not on the Grand River, not on Laurel Creek, and not in a ravine. It sits on well-drained Waterloo Moraine high ground. Anyone selling you a flood or creek angle here is inventing it. The real environmental story is canopy, debris load and retained moisture — plus about 160 cm of snow a year, roughly 44 cm in January, and snow on the ground around 95 days, which on a steep shaded Arts and Crafts slope means ice damming risk at the eaves unless the ventilation and eave protection are right.

Access, and a rare piece of good news

The missing hydro lines are an advantage — no service drops to work around. But unrestricted full-height trees, heritage streetlights, planted medians, minimal side yards and driveways running tight along the house all constrain boom reach and staging. Many of these homes were built before cars needed housing and had a single-car garage added to the side later, which narrows the approach further. We plan staging during the estimate rather than discovering it on the morning.

Every replacement is a full tear-off to the deck, with photographs of what's underneath before it's covered again. On a house that's been re-roofed five times, that documentation is the most valuable thing you get.

Quick Answers

Westmount roofing questions

Is Westmount in Kitchener or Waterloo?

Both, genuinely — and it was designed that way. Westmount was laid out in 1912–14 as a garden suburb deliberately straddling the boundary, and the City of Waterloo's own heritage study calls it a unique example of an upscale garden suburb spanning the Waterloo and Kitchener border, noting that some houses are physically located in both municipalities. Each city designates its own Westmount heritage landscape. Locals call the portion east of Westmount Road Old Westmount. One practical note: the road is Westmount Road East and West on the Kitchener side, and North and South on the Waterloo side. We serve all of it.

Why are Westmount roofs more complicated than newer homes?

Because of the architecture, and the City of Kitchener's heritage survey says so directly: rooflines here are often complex, reflecting the Arts and Crafts style, typically with one or more steep front gables intersecting a main gable roof. Residents also identified jerkinhead roofs — a clipped or half-hipped gable reflecting the area's German heritage — which is a genuinely distinctive Waterloo County form. Every intersection is a valley, and every valley is a flashing detail that has to be built correctly rather than caulked. That is why a Westmount roof takes longer and costs more than the same square footage on a simple gable.

How old are Westmount homes, and what does that mean for the roof?

The core east of Westmount Road developed primarily in the 1920s and 1930s, with some 1950s and 60s infill. Land west of Westmount Road wasn't registered until the 1950s, and the last stage — Inverness Drive and Huntley Crescent — was built in the early 1970s. So the oldest homes are 90 to 100 years old and are on their fourth, fifth or sixth roof. The shingle is rarely the interesting part on a house like that. What matters is the condition of decking that has taken multiple nailing cycles, century-old chimney and sidewall flashing details, and ventilation that was never designed to any modern standard.

The trees are enormous. Does that affect the roof?

Considerably, and Westmount's canopy is not accidental. The original deeds of sale required silver maples planted ten feet back from the street line and thirty feet apart, boulevard trees went in during the 1930s, and there is no overhead hydro on several streets, which the City notes has let the trees reach their full natural height. The medians on Rusholme, Union and Claremont create a double canopy. For a roof that means constant organic debris in valleys and gutters, moss and algae on shaded slopes, and slower drying after rain. Silver maples in particular are fast-growing and brittle, so limb drop directly over roof planes is a real consideration.

Are there access problems on Westmount's streets?

Some, and they're the opposite of what you'd expect. The absence of overhead hydro lines on Rusholme, Union, Glasgow, Dunbar, Claremont and Avondale is a real advantage — no service drops to work around. But it also means unrestricted full-height trees, and combined with heritage streetlights, planted medians, minimal side yards and driveways running tight alongside the houses, boom reach and truck staging need planning. Many of these homes were built before garages existed and had one added later to the side, which narrows things further.

My home is heritage designated. Can you still re-roof it?

Yes. Both cities recognise Westmount as a cultural heritage landscape, and there are individually designated properties on both sides of the boundary. Landscape-level recognition addresses the neighbourhood's character rather than ordinary re-roofing, but an individually designated property can carry specific requirements. We identify designation status before quoting, and where it applies we work to keep the roof's form, profile and visible detailing consistent with the original.
